# Pit: the animation director's verdict

Moment: Maya plays Move 3 and drives into the pit two spaces ahead. She is out until she reboots. Time budget 3.5 s.
The scenario calls it the classic Robo Rally disaster, and it happens a few times a game. Today the game shrinks
and spins her down the hole and leaves a red ring on the pit, all in about 2.2 s (`out/pit/baseline.sheet.jpg`).

I judged what is on screen in the final videos, not what the descriptions promise. I read every contact sheet,
then pulled frames from every video: strips at 10 fps across the whole moment, every frame (30 fps) around each
key beat (the rug pull, the separation, the impact, the camera moves, the drop), and full-resolution stills where
readability at TV size was in doubt. All times below are video times; each `play` starts at 0.6 s.

## Winner: 1b-show-off

It gets the biggest gasp-then-laugh in the set, and 1b tells it best: she clears the hole and poses for the sofa, then the hole lights its rim, visibly whips under her, she sinks still frozen in her pose, and the hole strolls home and glints, ending exactly on the game's state. A black square sliding across lit glass is the most readable motion a TV can show, and the piece breaks the rules for one beat and then puts them back, which is what the brief asks for.

**Porting:** Cut Theo down to one glance at the rug pull, because his running commentary splits the focus, and keep the card tag clamped inside the frame during the jump. Only play it when the board allows it: it needs the space before the pit on her path for the ramp, and a free floor space past the pit to land on (on the board, no wall in between, not a pit, no robot). Falls caused by a push or a belt, or a pit on her very first step, need a fallback, and 2a is that fallback. The moving hole is a topMat-cloned glass patch plus a sliding black plane, so the patch must match whatever the real board has under it (belt light, seams), and both must go the moment the hole is home, with the rim fading out rather than popping. At 3.33 s against a 3.5 s budget there is no slack: the jump has to replace the last segment of the move rather than follow it, and two falls in one register need queueing or a shorter pose hold.

## Runner-up: 2a-coat-check

The quietest piece in the set and the most Lumen: a pit is where light can't go, so her colour stays at the door as a crisp Pebble-shaped stain while her dark glass body drops in silence, and then the stain drains away along the seams. There is no gag to wear out, it works for any fall (driven, pushed or carried by a belt), and everything stays within a space or two. That makes it the right everyday version, and the right fallback wherever the springboard can't play.

**Porting:** Keep a specular highlight or a faint rim on the dark body so the drop still reads against the black hole at TV size, and keep the stain's hold short so nobody reads it as Maya still standing on (3,1). Soften the square clip around the stain, which reads as a selection box. Keep the threads short, away from pit edges and walls, and gone before the next register. The stain is built from copies of her ring, pool and eye while the real model is dimmed, so restore the model's own materials before she reboots.

## In short

Ship the springboard (1b) as the pit fall wherever the board has room for it, and use the coat check (2a) for every
fall the springboard can't stage (pushes, belts, a pit on the first step, pit fields, edges). If the springboard
wears thin after a few games, 2a can take over as the everyday version on its own. The pile-up (3a) is the next
best and the clearest to read. The lens versions (4b best) are a once-a-night treat at most, not the everyday pit.
